## Welcome / Why We Created This Guide

Providence Church has four Core Values: Gospel, Formation, Community, and Mission. These values help us describe the kind of disciples we want to become and the kind of church we want to be.

Over time, we realized that each Core Value needed practical focuses. These focuses help us take big ideas and bring them into everyday discipleship. We do not want Gospel, Formation, Community, and Mission to remain words on a wall. We want them to shape the way we trust Jesus, grow in Christ, love one another, and join God’s mission.

This guide was created to help Huddles walk through those focuses together. Each chapter is designed to give you Scripture to study, truth to consider, questions to discuss, and a challenge to practice.

The goal is not to finish a booklet. The goal is to become healthy disciples of Jesus.

---

## Why Should We Multiply Disciples?

**It’s Biblical.** Jesus promises to be with us and commands us to “go and make disciples” (Matthew 28:18–20). We can spend our time doing many great things: hosting parties, taking retreats, going to conferences, playing in sports tournaments, and inviting friends on Tuesday nights. Those things can be good and helpful, but the words of Jesus have not changed: “Go and make disciples.” Amid all our ministry activity, the command of Jesus keeps disciple-making at the center.

**It’s Influential.** Jesus says we are the “light of the world” (Matthew 5:14–16). Our light shines through how we live. Discipleship is accomplished not just through messengers, but also through models. As we spend time with one another, we notice each other’s lives, speech, decisions, habits, and responses. This means every interaction matters. In many ways, discipleship is influence, where things are caught and not just taught.

**It’s Transformational.** We live in a world that feeds us new lies and new teachings every day. We can no longer remain immature children. As disciples become familiar with the truth of God’s Word, they grow up into Christ instead of being carried along by every new teaching (Ephesians 4:14–15). Disciples are made through relationships. We extend grace to one another in sin and failure, but we also hold each other accountable as we work through hard things, apply God’s Word to our lives, and live by the strength of His Spirit.

**It’s Missional.** What we learn as disciples is incredibly valuable. As we learn to trust and obey God’s Word, we are called to teach others who will then teach others also (Acts 2:42; 2 Timothy 2:2). If every person intentionally discipled one other person and equipped that person to turn around and disciple someone else, our mission to multiply college students who are following and becoming like Jesus would spread across Omaha.

**It’s Loving.** Before Jesus spoke, He loved. He looked at people, loved them, empathized with them, and then spoke truth to them. We must make a conscious decision in our minds and hearts to love one another first. Discipleship relationships do not happen best in a classroom or professional environment, but in an honest, transparent, and gracious one. Compassion breeds discipleship (Mark 10:21; 1 Peter 4:8).

## How to Use This Guide

This guide is meant to be used in Huddles, or small groups of believers who want to grow. It can be used in one-on-one discipleship relationships, but the CPR portions may not feel as seamless.

Each chapter walks through one focus connected to one of Providence’s four Core Values. These Core Values are Gospel, Formation, Community, and Mission. The focuses do not perfectly summarize everything that could be said about each value, but they do serve as foundational markers. They help us build the basic practices, beliefs, and habits that should shape a healthy disciple.

For example, fasting is a beautiful spiritual discipline, but if someone is grounded in God’s Word and prayer, we believe they will eventually have a foundation for practices like fasting. Generosity and tithing are not separate focuses in this guide, but those themes will come up as we talk about worship, love, serving, and mission.

As these focuses are built into our lives, they create a foundation for other practices and rhythms of discipleship.

Each focus will include the following sections:

- **Artwork & Title** — Each focus begins by reminding us which Core Value it extends from. Slow down to take it in.
- **Definition** — Each focus includes a short definition. This gives clarity to what we mean and helps the group start with shared language.
- **Scripture & Quotes** — Each focus includes Scripture and quotes from voices throughout church history. These quotes remind us that we are not inventing something new. We are learning to follow Jesus with the saints who have gone before us.
- **Article** — Each focus includes an article to read together. The article is meant to define the focus, explain why it matters, and help you see how it connects to the Gospel and the Christian life.
- **SOAP** — Each focus includes four Scripture passages. Use the SOAP method to study the passages throughout the week. By looking at different texts, we will build a fuller understanding of each focus and see how the Bible speaks to it from multiple angles.
- **Discussion Questions** — Each focus includes discussion questions for your Huddle. Use these questions to talk honestly together. The goal of this time is not to give the most right-sounding answers, but to help one another understand the truth and apply it faithfully.
- **Challenge Options** — Each focus includes discipleship challenge options. Choose one option to complete each week. These challenges are designed to move the content from information to obedience.
- **Challenge Review** — Each focus ends with a challenge review. When you gather again, share which option you chose and how it went. Celebrate faithfulness, be honest about difficulty, and encourage each other to keep growing!

## How to SOAP

SOAP stands for **S**cripture, **O**bservations, **A**pplication, and **P**rayer.

Remember to pray as you open the Bible, asking God to show you the grace of Jesus.

### Scripture

Write down the passage of Scripture you are studying. Read the passage a few times, silently or out loud.

### Observations

Ask questions about Scripture that will help you understand its meaning. Note thoughts that might be significant.

Questions to ask:

- What does the text mean?
- What do I learn about God?
- What do I learn about people or myself?
- Is there anything I do not understand?
- Is there anything that challenges or convicts me?
- How does this passage connect to the Gospel?
- How does this passage connect to the focus we are studying?

### Application

Answer the question:

> No matter where I am spiritually, what would it look like for me to apply this Scripture this week, based on what I read and the observations I made?

Write out your response to this question.

### Prayer

Ask that God would be glorified and that He would help you as you seek to apply the passage this week.

This type of Bible study aims to grow us in faith and obedience, not knowledge alone.

## Huddle Rhythms

Each Huddle embodies the Core Values. The Gospel is woven through everything, and we seek to live out Formation, Community, and Mission together.

> **Note:** In Huddle Rhythms, the order of living out the Core Values does not match the Providence diamond order. Instead, they are arranged in the most practical order.

As you go through these rhythms, pay attention to those who are sharing. Put your phones down, and listen to what they are saying. Try not to interrupt, but feel free to ask clarifying questions so you can make sure you understand them! When walking through the 5 C’s, you might feel tempted to just share with the Huddle leader, but we want to encourage you to engage with everyone in the Huddle.

### Core Value: Community

Begin by sharing how your last week has been. We use the 5 C’s structure: Communion, Community, Crush, Confession, and Celebrate.

- **Communion** — Share how your relationship with God has been this week.
- **Community** — Share what has been happening in your life and how you are really doing.
- **Crush** — Share honestly about relationships, attraction, dating, sexuality, or places where you are tempted to seek comfort or validation outside of Christ.
- **Confession** — Share how you have sinned against God this past week. Be specific and honest. After someone confesses, the next person prays for them, and the next person restores them with Scripture. This helps us respond to sin with grace, truth, and the Gospel.

  > **Note:** What is shared in a Huddle should stay in the Huddle unless it is essential for someone else to know. For example, if someone’s life is in danger, tell a staff member.

- **Celebrate** — Share where you have seen God’s grace this week.

### Core Value: Formation

Take time to read the article for the week together. Then review your SOAP passages and discuss the questions. Point out what stood out to you, what God revealed to you, and what convicted or encouraged you in your walk with Jesus.

The goal is not just to complete the content, but to be formed by God’s Word and to help one another keep following Jesus.

### Core Value: Mission

Review the weekly challenge together. Talk about what obedience could look like this week and how each person plans to respond.

Then pray for one another. Ask God to help you live out what you are learning with faith, humility, and dependence on the Holy Spirit.

## A Call to Faithfulness

A Huddle is not meant to be a meeting you attend once a week and then forget about. It is meant to be life-on-life discipleship. The weekly gathering matters, but you could answer questions, read the content, and still miss the aim of discipleship. The goal is to help one another follow Jesus in real life. So be faithful and do your best with the guide, both for your own discipleship and for the discipleship of the people you are meeting with.

This also means Huddle members should pursue one another outside of the weekly meeting. Text each other. Pray for each other. Ask follow-up questions. Spend time together. Celebrate obedience. Check in after hard confessions. Help each other take next steps. Discipleship happens in the room, but it also happens in the car, over coffee, after church, between classes, and throughout the week.

Faithfulness matters. If you commit to a Huddle, show up. Come prepared. Do the readings. Complete the SOAP passages. Be honest during confession. Pray for your group. Follow through on the challenge. Treat your Huddle like it matters! Your presence, honesty, and consistency will help shape the people around you.

Faithfulness also means moving toward others when it would be easier to drift. If someone misses Huddle, reach out. If someone confesses something heavy, follow up. If someone is discouraged, encourage them. If someone is taking a step of obedience, celebrate with them. Do not wait for someone else to build the culture. Help create it.

The goal is not perfection. Everyone will have busy weeks, tired weeks, and weeks where they feel weak. But Gospel Community is built through ordinary faithfulness over time. Keep showing up. Keep telling the truth. Keep praying. Keep pointing one another to Jesus.

A Huddle is a small group of people choosing to take responsibility for one another’s discipleship. By God’s grace, be faithful to the people He has placed in front of you.

## Gospel: Saved by Jesus

*[CORE VALUE ARTWORK INSERT: GOSPEL]*

The Gospel is the good news of Jesus’ life, death, and resurrection. Through the Gospel, we are forgiven of our sins and brought into relationship with God by Trusting Jesus as our Lord and Savior. In response, we desire to clearly understand and articulate what He has done for us, and continually apply that truth to our lives, especially when we sin.

### Placement & Purpose

The Gospel is the foundation of the Christian life. We do not begin with our effort, obedience, growth, community, or mission. We begin with what Jesus has done for us.

The down arrow reminds us that God came down to us in Christ. We did not climb our way up to God. Jesus entered into our world, lived the perfect life we failed to live, died the death we deserved to die, and rose again so sinners could be forgiven and brought into relationship with God.

Because of this, healthy disciples must return to the Gospel again and again. We accept the Gospel by trusting Jesus as Savior and Lord. We articulate the Gospel so we can understand and share the good news clearly. We apply the Gospel to our lives, especially when we sin, so that we respond with repentance, faith, and hope instead of hiding, shame, or self-righteousness.

- **Start in the Gospel.** Everything else flows from the Gospel.
- **Live in the Gospel.** Do not forget the cornerstone of your life.
- **End in the Gospel.** The same grace that first saved you will carry you home.

---

### Accept

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: ACCEPT]*

#### Definition

To accept the Gospel means to trust Jesus as Savior and Lord. We receive the good news by faith, believing that Jesus is who He says He is and that He has done what we could never do for ourselves.

#### Quotes

> “Now faith means believing what you don’t yet see, and the reward of this faith is to see what you believe.”
>
> — Augustine, *Sermon 43*, c. 400

> “Faith is a firm and certain knowledge of God’s benevolence toward us, founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in Christ, both revealed to our minds and sealed upon our hearts by the Holy Spirit.”
>
> — John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, 1559

> “Assurance of salvation is the fruit of faith in Christ. Christ is able to and does, in fact, save all those who come to him through faith.”
>
> — Sinclair Ferguson, *The Whole Christ*, 2016

#### Article

The Gospel is not first something we achieve, improve, or prove. The word gospel means “good news.” It is good news that must be received. To accept the Gospel means to trust that Jesus is who He says He is and that He has done what we could never do for ourselves. We believe Jesus to be both Lord and Savior.

We are not saved by being good enough, religious enough, obedient enough, or impressive enough. We are saved by grace, which means we are not the ones who do the saving work. In response to that grace, we trust in Jesus as our Lord, continuing to believe that He is good and worthy of our entire lives.

Why do we need to be saved? Every person has sinned against God and stands in need of rescue. Sin is more than making mistakes or having weaknesses. Sin is a direct rebellion against the God who made us, loves us, and rules over all things. Because of sin, we are separated from God and unable to fix ourselves. But God, in His mercy, sent His Son. Jesus lived the perfect life we failed to live, died the death we deserved to die, and rose again so sinners could be forgiven and brought into relationship with God.

To accept the Gospel is to place our faith in Jesus as both Savior and Lord. As Savior, Jesus rescues us from the penalty of sin. His death on the cross is enough to forgive us completely. We do not add our good works, church attendance, baptism, or spiritual effort to His finished work. We receive salvation as a gift.

As Lord, Jesus becomes the One we follow, trust, and obey. We cannot treat Jesus as a ticket to heaven while refusing His authority over our lives. True faith receives all of Christ.

This means saving faith is not just agreeing with historical facts about Jesus. Someone can know Christian truths, understand Bible stories, and even believe that Jesus died and rose again without truly trusting Him. Faith is personal dependence. It means to trust. It is saying, “Jesus, You are my only hope. I am trusting You to save me, forgive me, and lead me.” To accept Jesus is to trust Him.

This trust does not leave us as we are. As we entrust our lives to Christ, He turns us from sin and draws us into the freedom of repentance. Repentance does not mean we clean ourselves up before coming to Jesus. It means we turn from sin and turn toward Him. We come honestly, not pretending we are better than we are, and we trust that His grace is greater than our failure. Faith and repentance belong together because trusting Jesus for eternity also means trusting Him with our lives today.

Accepting the Gospel gives Christians confidence. Our salvation does not rest on how strongly we feel, how well we perform, or how consistent we have been. Our salvation rests on Christ. John 10 says that Jesus knows His sheep, gives them eternal life, and no one will snatch them out of His hand. That is our hope.

That’s why we must begin here. Before we articulate the Gospel, apply the Gospel, grow in formation, live in community, or join the mission of God, we must first receive what Jesus has done. We accept the Gospel by faith, and we keep living by that same faith every day. To accept Jesus is to entrust our whole lives to Him as Savior and Lord. In Him, guilty people find forgiveness, weary people find hope, and sinners find a King who is worthy of their trust. It is the most beautiful decision we could ever make.

### Articulate

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: ARTICULATE]*

#### Definition

To articulate the Gospel means to clearly explain the good news of Jesus in a way that is faithful to Scripture and understandable to ourselves and others.

#### Quotes

> “Here, then… is our offering to you who love Christ, a brief statement of the faith of Christ and of the manifestation of His Godhead to us. This will give you a beginning, and you must go on to prove its truth by the study of the Scriptures.”
>
> — Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*, 318 AD

> “We believe that this Holy Scripture contains the will of God completely and that everything one must believe to be saved is sufficiently taught in it.”
>
> — Belgic Confession, Article 7, 1561

> “The gospel is not good instructions, not a good idea, and not good advice. The gospel is an announcement of what God has done for us in Jesus Christ.”
>
> — Michael Horton, “Christ at the Center,” 2009

#### Article

Christians should be able to clearly explain the Gospel. The Gospel is not vague spiritual advice, a general message about being a better person, or a reminder that God loves everyone. The Gospel is the good news of what God has done through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ to save sinners and bring them into relationship with Himself.

Being able to articulate the Gospel matters first for our own faith. If we cannot explain what we believe, it may be difficult to know whether we actually understand it. If someone says, “I am saved by Jesus,” but does not know how that happens, there may be reason to grow in clarity and assurance. Clear articulation helps us remember where our confidence rests. We are not saved because we are sincere, moral, religious, or emotionally moved. We are saved because Jesus lived for us, died for us, rose for us, and calls us to respond in faith.

Being able to articulate the Gospel also matters because we are called to share it with others. People around us have different assumptions about God, sin, Jesus, truth, salvation, and eternity. Some think Christianity is mainly about rules. Others think all religions are basically the same. Others assume God will accept them if they are good enough. If we love people, we should want to speak clearly about the only message that can save them.

One helpful way to explain the Gospel is through four movements: Ruler, Rebellion, Rescue, and Response.

- **Ruler.** God is the Ruler. He created all things, owns all things, and defines what is good.
- **Rebellion.** Humanity has responded with Rebellion. We have sinned against God, rejected His rule, and deserve judgment.
- **Rescue.** But God provided Rescue through Jesus. Jesus lived without sin, died in the place of sinners, and rose again in victory over sin and death.
- **Response.** Now God calls for a Response. Christ says repent and believe in Jesus as Savior and Lord.

There are other faithful ways to articulate the Gospel as well. Some use Creation, Fall, Redemption, and Restoration. Others use passages like 1 Corinthians 15, Romans 3, Ephesians 2, or Titus 3. The goal is not to memorize one perfect script. The goal is to understand the Gospel so deeply that we can explain it truthfully, clearly, and personally. We never change the essentials of the Gospel to fit the person in front of us. But as we understand it more deeply, we learn to show how the same good news speaks to different questions, fears, wounds, and hopes.

Good Gospel articulation should be both accurate and understandable. We do not want to remove hard truths like sin, judgment, repentance, or the cross. But we also do not want to hide the Gospel behind confusing language. It is not helpful to explain biblical truth in words people cannot understand.

Those who place their faith in Christ will desire to grow in their ability to articulate the Gospel. This takes practice. It may feel awkward at first, but clarity grows as we speak. Practice with your Huddle, a friend, a leader, or even someone who does not yet believe. Ask what was clear and what was confusing. Keep learning Scripture. Keep rehearsing the good news.

The Gospel is too precious to leave blurry. God has given us good news worth understanding, treasuring, and speaking. We articulate the Gospel because Christ is worthy, people are eternal, and the message of Jesus is the power of God for salvation.

### Apply

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: APPLY]*

#### Definition

To apply the Gospel means to bring the truth of what Jesus has done into the real places of our lives, especially when we sin, fail, feel ashamed, or drift from God.

#### Quotes

> “Let us fix our thoughts on the blood of Christ; and reflect how precious that blood is in God’s eyes, inasmuch as its outpouring for our salvation has opened the grace of repentance to all mankind.”
>
> — Clement of Rome, *1 Clement*, c. 96 AD

> “If we have this for a foundation truth, that there is more mercy in Christ than sin in us, there can be no danger in thorough dealing.”
>
> — Richard Sibbes, *The Bruised Reed*, 1630

> “The gospel is not just the ABCs but the A to Z of Christianity. The gospel is not the minimum required doctrine necessary to enter the kingdom but the way we make all progress in the kingdom.”
>
> — Tim Keller, “The Centrality of the Gospel,” 2000

#### Article

The Gospel is not only the message that saves us at the beginning of the Christian life. The Gospel is the truth we return to every day. To apply the Gospel means we take what Jesus has done and bring it into the real places of our lives, especially when we sin, fail, feel ashamed, or drift from God.

When Christians sin, we often respond in one of two unhealthy ways. Some of us minimize sin. We excuse it, hide it, compare it to something worse, or tell ourselves it is not a big deal. But sin is a big deal. Sin is rebellion against God. Sin damages our relationship with Him and with others. Sin is serious enough that Jesus had to die for it. Applying the Gospel means we do not treat lightly what Christ carried to the cross.

Others of us maximize sin. We spiral into shame, self-hatred, and despair. We believe our sin is bigger than God’s mercy. We assume God is tired of us, disappointed in us, or unwilling to receive us again. But the Gospel tells us that Jesus did not die for a cleaned-up version of us. He died for sinners. Romans 8:1 says, “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” Applying the Gospel means we do not treat our sin as more powerful than Christ’s blood.

The Gospel teaches us to take sin seriously without losing hope. We can confess honestly because we are already loved completely in Christ. We can repent deeply because we are not trying to earn our way back to God. We can come into the light because Jesus has already carried our shame.

To apply the Gospel is to preach truth to ourselves when our hearts believe lies. When we think, “I have to hide,” the Gospel says, “You are fully known and fully loved in Christ.” When we think, “I need to prove myself,” the Gospel says, “Jesus has already secured your standing before God.” When we think, “I am too far gone,” the Gospel says, “Christ came to save sinners.”

Applying the Gospel also changes the way we respond to others. When we understand and experience the grace and mercy of Christ, this will impact the way we forgive those who sin against us. This doesn’t mean we ignore sin, avoid hard conversations, or pretend hurt does not matter. It means we move toward others with both truth and grace, remembering how Christ has moved toward us.

Someone grounded in the good news of Jesus will seek to return to the Gospel quickly. Not apathetically, as if sin does not matter, and not despairingly, as if grace is not enough. We return with honesty, repentance, faith, and hope. The Christian life is not about pretending we never fail. It is about learning, again and again, to bring our failures to Jesus.

The Gospel is not just what we believe. It is what we apply. In our guilt, shame, temptation, relationships, obedience, and repentance, we keep coming back to Christ. He is enough for our worst moments, our weakest days, and our deepest failures.

## Formation: Changed by Jesus

*[CORE VALUE ARTWORK INSERT: FORMATION]*

Formation is the lifelong process of being shaped to look more like Jesus. God forms us as we regularly read His Word to know Him, pray in dependence on Him, and worship Him together with His people. Through these rhythms, we grow in faith, deepen our trust in God, and are transformed over time by His presence and power.

### Placement & Purpose

After the Gospel brings us into relationship with God, He does not leave us unchanged. He begins to form us by His presence and power.

The up arrow reminds us that disciples are meant to grow up into Christ. Formation is not self-improvement, behavior modification, or trying harder to become a better person. Formation is God shaping us over time through the ordinary means of grace.

God forms us as we read His Word, pray in dependence on Him, and worship together with His people. These rhythms do not earn God’s love. They help us live in the love we have already received through Jesus.

We do not remain stagnant. To be a healthy follower of Christ is to be formed by God to know Him, trust Him, obey Him, and reflect Him more and more.

---

### Read

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: READ]*

#### Definition

To read means to regularly receive God’s Word so that we may know Him, trust Him, obey Him, and be formed by Him.

#### Quotes

> “Being most properly assured that the Scriptures are indeed perfect, since they were spoken by the Word of God and His Spirit.”
>
> — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, c. 180 AD

> “He who wants to hear God speak should read Holy Scripture.”
>
> — Martin Luther, *Against the Roman Papacy, an Institution of the Devil* (1545)

> “If indeed the Bible is the Word of God, nothing could be more arrogant. It is God who corrects us; we don’t correct Him. We do not stand over God but under Him.”
>
> — R. C. Sproul, *Five Things Every Christian Needs to Grow*, 2008

#### Article

God forms His people through His Word. If we want to know God, hear from God, trust God, and obey God, we cannot separate those desires from Scripture. The Bible is not an ordinary religious book, a collection of wise thoughts, or a tool for inspiration. It is the Word of God, given so that we would know Him and be shaped by Him.

Reading Scripture matters because God has spoken. We are not left to guess what God is like, what He has done, what He loves, what He commands, or what He promises. In His Word, God reveals Himself. He shows us His holiness, His mercy, His justice, His patience, His faithfulness, and most clearly, His Son. The whole Bible leads us to Jesus, who is the fullest revelation of God.

This means reading the Bible is not mainly about gaining information. It is about formation. We read to know God, and as we know God, we are changed. Scripture confronts our sin, corrects our false beliefs, strengthens our faith, comforts us in suffering, and trains us to walk in obedience. Hebrews 4 says the Word of God is living and active. It does not sit lifeless on the page. By the power of the Spirit, God uses His Word to expose, heal, and transform us.

But reading Scripture rightly requires humility. We do not come to the Bible standing over it as judges; we come under it as people who need to be taught. Our culture, emotions, preferences, and experiences are not the final authority–God’s Word is. This does not mean every passage will be easy to understand or simple to apply. Some parts of Scripture require patience, study, and help from others, but we trust that God’s Word is good, true, and necessary.

Reading consistently is also important. No single moment in Scripture has to feel dramatic for God to be forming us. Much of formation happens slowly. Day after day, passage after passage, God renews our minds and reshapes our loves. Just as unhealthy voices can gradually deform us, God’s Word steadily reforms us.

Someone who wants to know God will learn to read Scripture with intentionality and look for opportunities to respond. We ask, “What does this passage say?” “What does it teach me about God?” “What does it reveal about people?” “Is there a command to obey, a promise to trust, a sin to confess, or a truth to believe?” We do not read to check off our to-do list. We read to meet with God and respond to Him.

The Gospel keeps us from reading Scripture as a way to prove ourselves. We do not read so God will love us. We read because, in Christ, God already has. Scripture is not a ladder we climb to reach God. It is one of the primary ways God comes near to us, speaks to us, and forms us into the image of Jesus.

### Pray

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: PRAY]*

#### Definition

To pray means to speak to God in dependence, trust, confession, gratitude, and need as children who have been brought near to the Father through Jesus.

#### Quotes

> “If you are such, take courage to call out to God with those beloved words and to name the Lord of all as your own Father. He will look upon you with fatherly eyes.”
>
> — Gregory of Nyssa, “Homily 2 on the Lord’s Prayer,” c. 385 AD

> “Prayer is a sincere, sensible, affectionate pouring out of the heart or soul to God, through Christ, in the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit.”
>
> — John Bunyan, *A Discourse Touching Prayer*, 1662

> “Self-will and prayer are both ways of getting things done. At the center of self-will is me, carving a world in my image, but at the center of prayer is God, carving me in his Son’s image.”
>
> — Paul E. Miller, *A Praying Life*, 2009

#### Article

Prayer is how Christians speak to God in dependence, trust, confession, gratitude, and need. It is not a performance, a formula, or a way to impress God with spiritual language. Prayer is the response of children who have been brought near to their Father through Jesus Christ.

Prayer matters because the Christian life cannot be lived in our own strength. We are dependent creatures. We need wisdom, mercy, endurance, courage, forgiveness, and help. Prayer teaches us to stop living as if we are self-sufficient. When we pray, we admit that God is God, and we are not.

Jesus assumed His people would pray. He did not say, “If you pray,” but “when you pray.” He taught His disciples to come to God as Father, to honor His name, to seek His kingdom, to ask for daily bread, to confess sin, and to plead for deliverance from temptation. Prayer is not detached from real life. It is where our real needs, fears, sins, desires, and burdens are brought honestly before God.

Prayer also forms us. Often, we come to prayer wanting God to change our circumstances. Sometimes He does. But in prayer, God also changes us. He reshapes our desires, exposes our motives, quiets our anxiety, strengthens our faith, and aligns our hearts with His will. Prayer is not simply how we get things from God. It is one of the primary ways we grow in relationship with Him.

This means prayer gets to be honest. We do not need to pretend before God. The Psalms teach us to bring grief, fear, confusion, joy, repentance, and praise before Him. God is not helped by fake prayers. He is not honored by spiritual language that hides what is actually happening in our hearts. Because Jesus has opened the way to the Father, we can come with confidence, not because we are impressive, but because Christ is our mediator.

It’s also an invitation to pray consistently and persistently! Many Christians struggle because prayer does not always feel powerful in the moment. Sometimes it feels ordinary. Sometimes our minds wander. Sometimes we do not know what to say. But prayer is not made meaningful by how intense we feel. Prayer is meaningful because God hears His people. We keep praying because God is faithful, not because prayer always feels easy.

A healthy Christian learns to pray both privately and with others. Private prayer deepens our personal dependence on God. Praying with others teaches us to carry burdens together, celebrate God’s work together, and seek His help as a community. Both matter.

The Gospel keeps prayer from becoming either prideful or hopeless. We do not pray to earn access to God. Jesus has already secured that access. We also do not avoid prayer because we feel unworthy. Jesus invites sinners, sufferers, doubters, and weak people to come. Prayer is not a place for impressive Christians. It is a place for needy Christians to meet with a gracious Father.

### Worship

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: WORSHIP]*

#### Definition

To worship means to glorify God with all of life, especially as we gather with His people to hear His Word, pray, sing, receive His grace, and respond to Him together.

#### Quotes

> “They met on a stated day before it was light, and addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity.”
>
> — Pliny the Younger, *Letter to Trajan*, c. 112 AD

> “The reading of the Scriptures with godly fear; the sound preaching, and conscionable hearing of the word… singing of psalms with grace in the heart… are all parts of the ordinary religious worship of God.”
>
> — *Westminster Confession of Faith*, Chapter 21.5, 1646

> “The whole gathering is ‘worship’, not just the singing and music. In the preaching, prayers, and everything in between, God ministers to and through the whole congregation for his own glory.”
>
> — Matt Merker, *Corporate Worship*, 2021

#### Article

Worship is bigger than singing, but one of the clearest ways Christians worship together is by gathering with the church to hear God’s Word, pray, receive the sacraments, and sing. Corporate worship is not a concert, a warm-up for the sermon, or a weekly religious event. It is the gathered people of God responding to the glory and grace of God.

God forms His people when they gather. Throughout Scripture, God calls His people together to remember who He is, hear what He has said, confess their need, receive His grace, and respond with praise. We were not made to follow Jesus in isolation. When the church gathers, we are reminded that we belong to a people, not just a private faith.

Singing is a major part of corporate worship because God has commanded it and because it forms us in a unique way. Colossians 3:16 tells believers to let the word of Christ dwell richly among them by singing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs. When we sing, we are not only expressing what we feel. We are teaching and admonishing one another with truth. The discouraged Christian hears the church sing of God’s faithfulness. The tempted Christian hears the church sing of Christ’s worth. The suffering Christian hears the church sing of future hope.

This means our singing is not mainly about personal preference. Some songs may fit our style better than others, but corporate worship is not designed around individual taste. We sing truths that are biblical, rich, and centered on God. We sing because God is worthy, because the church needs to hear the truth, and because our own hearts need to be reminded.

Corporate worship also teaches humility. We gather under God’s Word. We are not there to stand over Scripture, judge every element by our preferences, or consume a spiritual product. We are there to receive. In preaching, God’s Word confronts, comforts, corrects, and forms us. In prayer, we confess dependence together. In singing, we join our voices with the body of Christ. In all of it, God is shaping us as His people.

The Gospel is central to corporate worship. We do not gather because we have performed well enough during the week. We gather because Jesus has opened the way to God. We come as forgiven sinners, needy saints, adopted children, and members of one body. Our worship is not an attempt to earn God’s presence. It is a response to the God who has already come near to us in Christ.

We gather with the church not because attendance can save us, but because God has chosen to meet and form His people there. We need the Word preached. We need to sing with the saints. We need to remember the Gospel. Week after week, God uses the gathered church to remind us that our small lives belong to His much greater story.

Corporate worship trains us to see God as worthy. Week after week, we gather, listen, confess, sing, and respond. Over time, God uses ordinary worship to shape ordinary people into worshipers who love Him with all of their lives.

Because Jesus has loved us, we learn to love one another. Because Jesus has covered our sin, we learn to confess honestly. Because Jesus speaks grace and truth over us, we learn to encourage each other toward faithfulness.

#### Definition

To love means to sacrificially seek the good of others because Christ has first loved us.

> “The doctrine of grace creates a culture of grace. When the doctrine is clear and the culture is beautiful, that church will be powerful.”
>
> — Ray Ortlund, *Gospel*, 2014

#### Article

Christian community is formed by the love of Christ. We do not love one another because it is easy, convenient, or natural. We love because God has first loved us. Through the Gospel, God brings sinners into His family and teaches us to care for one another as brothers and sisters in Christ.

Biblical love is more than affection. It is not simply liking someone, having similar interests, or enjoying their personality. Love is a commitment to seek another person’s good, even when it costs us. Jesus shows us this kind of love most clearly. He did not love us from a distance. He came near, bore our burdens, gave Himself for us, and laid down His life.

This means love in the church must become tangible. It should show up in real acts of care, patience, generosity, sacrifice, and presence. Love listens when someone is hurting. Love brings meals when someone is overwhelmed. Love helps meet needs. Love moves toward people who are easy to overlook. Love makes time. Love carries burdens that are too heavy for someone to carry alone.

The early church gives us a picture of this kind of community. In Acts 2, believers devoted themselves to teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer. They shared what they had, cared for those in need, and lived with glad and generous hearts. Their love for one another was not detached from their witness. As the church cared for one another, the watching world saw something different.

This kind of love requires humility. In community, we will be sinned against, inconvenienced, misunderstood, and disappointed. People will have needs at inconvenient times. Some people will be harder for us to love than others. But the Gospel reminds us that Jesus loved us when we were not lovely. He moved toward us when we were weak, sinful, and undeserving. His love becomes the pattern and power for our love.

Love also requires wisdom. Loving someone does not mean ignoring boundaries, enabling sin, or trying to become everyone’s savior. We are not Jesus. We have limits. Sometimes love means helping directly. Sometimes love means bringing others in. Sometimes love means speaking truth, asking hard questions, or encouraging someone to receive help. Christian love holds sacrifice and wisdom together.

Followers of Jesus do not wait for community to become loving. They help create the loving community. This begins with ordinary faithfulness: noticing needs, asking questions, following up, showing hospitality, and being willing to be interrupted for the good of others.

The Gospel makes this possible. We have been known, loved, and cared for by Christ. Now we get to reflect His love within His family. A community shaped by the Gospel becomes a place where people do not just say they love one another, but actually bear burdens, meet needs, and lay down their lives for the good of the body.

### Confess

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: CONFESS]*

#### Definition

To confess means to bring sin honestly into the light before God and trusted believers so that we may receive prayer, restoration, and healing through the Gospel.

#### Quotes

> “The Lord knows all things, but He waits for your words, not that He may punish, but that He may pardon.”
>
> — Ambrose of Milan, *Concerning Repentance* 2.7.53 (384 AD)

> “God is honoured by confession of sin every way. It honours his omniscience.”
>
> — Richard Sibbes, “The Church’s Complaint and Confidence”, 1636

> “In confession the break-through to community takes place.”
>
> — Dietrich Bonhoeffer, *Life Together*, 112, (1939)

#### Article

One of the clearest signs that someone understands the Gospel is that they are willing to bring their sin into the light. Christians do not confess because we are trying to earn forgiveness. We confess because, in Jesus, forgiveness has already been secured. The cross frees us from pretending, hiding, minimizing, and excusing. Because Jesus has paid for our sin, we can be honest about our sin.

Confession begins with God. When we sin, our first move should be to come honestly before Him. Psalm 32 describes the misery of hidden sin: “when I kept silent, my bones wasted away.” But when David finally confessed, he experienced the mercy of God: “I acknowledged my sin to you… and you forgave the iniquity of my sin.” Sin grows in secrecy, but healing begins in the light.

But the Bible does not only call us to confess to God. James 5:16 says, “Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed.” This does not mean another Christian has the power to forgive us in God’s place. Only God can forgive sin. But God often uses His people to help us experience the healing, restoration, accountability, and encouragement we need.

When we confess to another Christian, the truths of the Gospel often become tangible. Someone sits across from us, hears the thing we were afraid to say out loud, prays for us, and reminds us of what Jesus has done. Grace is no longer something we only know in our heads. We experience it within the family of God.

Confession is one of the ways Christian community becomes real. Without confession, community can easily stay at the surface. We can talk about school, work, relationships, church, and everything else while hiding the places where we are actually struggling. But when believers confess honestly, they remind one another that no one is saved by appearing strong. We are saved by the grace of Jesus.

Biblical confession names sin truthfully. It refuses to minimize sin by saying, “It was not that big of a deal.” It also refuses to maximize sin by saying, “I am too far gone for grace.” The Gospel teaches us to take sin seriously without believing that our sin is stronger than Jesus.

At Providence, we often describe healthy confession through CPR: Confess, Pray, Restore.

1. **Confess.** First, we confess honestly. We name our sin without excuses and bring it into the light.
2. **Pray.** Then, we pray. We bring that person and their sin before God together, asking for mercy, strength, repentance, and freedom.
3. **Restore.** Finally, we restore. Restoration means speaking the truth of the Gospel back over the person who confessed. Before we rush toward advice, correction, or a plan for what they should do next, we remind them of what is true because of Jesus. Their sin is serious, but Christ has paid for it. Their failure does not remove them from the family of God. There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. They can repent, get back up, and keep walking with Him.

This matters because our natural response to someone’s confession can easily become shock, shame, interrogation, or immediate advice. But when someone has already admitted that they are wrong, they usually do not need us to begin by telling them again that they are wrong. They need the Gospel.

That does not mean we never give advice or help someone take practical steps. Restoration may eventually include accountability, removing access to temptation, making something right with another person, or asking for additional help. Those conversations matter. But let the good news of Jesus rest on someone before you begin trying to fix them.

Imagine someone says, “I need to confess that I watched pornography last night. I knew I needed to stop, but I chose not to.”

A healthy response could begin simply: “Thank you for telling us. Let me pray for you.”

Then someone else can open Scripture and remind them, “Jesus knew this sin when He went to the cross. If you are in Christ, this does not change His love for you or your standing before God. There is no condemnation for you. Confess it, receive His mercy, and keep walking in the light.” This is the practice of CPR.

The Gospel makes Christian confession both honest and hopeful. Honest, because sin really does matter. Hopeful, because Jesus really is gentle with sinners who come to Him. The goal of confession is not embarrassment. The goal is freedom.

In a community shaped by the Gospel, confession can become regular and normal. We do not have to hide. We do not have to pretend. We can confess our sins because Christ has already carried our shame, paid our debt, and invited us into the light.

### Encourage

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: ENCOURAGE]*

#### Definition

To encourage means to speak grace and truth in a way that strengthens faith, stirs up love, and helps others keep following Jesus.

#### Quotes

> “For as iron sharpens iron, so also association increases love.”
>
> — John Chrysostom, “Homily 19 on Hebrews,” c. 390 AD

> “It is their duty in their mutual watch over one another, to exhort each other unto holiness and perseverance.”
>
> — John Owen, *A Brief Instruction in the Worship of God*, 1667

> “Biblical encouragement isn’t focused on complimenting someone’s haircut or telling them how good their homemade salsa tastes. The encouragement the Scriptures refer to is explicitly Christian encouragement.”
>
> — Garrett Kell, “Encourage One Another: Giving Grace with Your Words,” 2014

#### Article

Encouragement is one of the most ordinary and powerful ways Christians build one another up. Every person knows what it feels like to be tired, discouraged, insecure, tempted, or spiritually dull. Because of this, God calls His people to speak words that strengthen faith, stir up love, and help one another keep following Jesus. To encourage is to literally give someone else courage.

Biblical encouragement is more than compliments. Compliments can be good, but encouragement goes deeper. A compliment says, “You did a great job.” Encouragement says, “I see God’s grace at work in you.” A compliment may notice someone’s gifts, but encouragement helps them see how those gifts can be used for Christ. A compliment can make someone feel seen, but Gospel encouragement helps someone remember who they are in Jesus.

The Bible repeatedly commands Christians to encourage one another. Hebrews 10:24–25 says, “Let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works… encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near.” This means encouragement is not optional for the Christian community. We are called to pay attention to one another. We are meant to consider, notice, and think carefully about how to help each other keep going.

Encouragement matters because the Christian life is difficult. Sin is deceitful. Suffering is exhausting. The world is distracting. Our own hearts are often weak. Without encouragement, people can begin to drift, isolate, or believe lies about God and themselves. But when Christians speak truth in love, God uses those words to strengthen His people.

The most helpful encouragement is specific, Christ-centered, and sincere. Specific encouragement names what we actually see. Instead of only saying, “You’re awesome,” we might say, “I saw the way you patiently listened to that person, and it reminded me of the gentleness of Jesus.” Christ-centered encouragement connects what we see in others to the work of God. Sincere encouragement is not flattery or manipulation. It is honest speech meant to build someone up.

Encouragement also helps create a healthy community. In a discouraging culture, people often compare, criticize, compete, or tear one another down. But the church should be different. Because God has spoken grace over us in Christ, we should be quick to speak grace over one another. We do not encourage people because they are perfect. We encourage them because God is at work in them.

This means encouragement is not only for naturally affirming people. Some people find words of encouragement easy. Others may feel awkward or unsure of what to say. But every Christian can grow in encouragement by paying attention, praying for others, and naming evidences of grace when they see them.

A community shaped by the Gospel is filled with words that give life. We want to be people who notice God’s work in one another and say it out loud. We want to strengthen the weak, comfort the discouraged, stir up the passive, and remind one another of Christ. Encouragement is not small. It is one of the ways God keeps His people faithful.

## Mission: Sent by Jesus

*[CORE VALUE ARTWORK INSERT: MISSION]*

Mission is our call to join God in what He is doing in the world. We live on mission by preaching the Gospel to those who do not know Jesus, walking with others as they grow as disciples, and serving faithfully in the church and beyond. As we go, we seek to make Christ known and to reflect His love in both word and deed.

### Placement & Purpose

Mission is the ‘right’ or ‘out’ arrow because God sends His people outward to join Him in what He is doing in the world. The Gospel does not stop with us. Jesus saves us, forms us, brings us into community, and then sends us out as witnesses, disciple-makers, and servants.

Mission is not only for pastors, missionaries, or especially bold Christians. Every disciple of Jesus is called to live on mission. We proclaim the Gospel to those who do not yet know Christ. We walk with others as they grow in faith. We serve faithfully in the church and beyond so that Christ would be made known in word and deed.

The grace of Christ never stops with the person it saves. Jesus welcomes us, transforms us, and sends us. We live on mission because Christ is worthy to be known, people are made for eternity, and God is gathering a people for His name.

### Evangelize

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: EVANGELIZE]*

#### Definition

To evangelize means to share the good news of Jesus with those who do not yet know Him, calling them to respond in repentance and faith.

#### Quotes

> “Let us not be ashamed to confess the Crucified. Let the cross, as our seal, be boldly made with our fingers upon our brow and on all occasions.”
>
> — Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lectures*, c. 350 AD

> “I preached, as never sure to preach again, and as a dying man to dying men!”
>
> — Richard Baxter, *Poetical Fragments*, 1681

> “According to the New Testament, evangelism is just preaching the gospel, the evangel. It is a work of communication in which Christians make themselves mouth-pieces for God’s message of mercy to sinners.”
>
> — J. I. Packer, *Evangelism and the Sovereignty of God*, 1961

#### Article

Evangelism is the act of sharing the good news of Jesus with those who do not yet know Him. It is not merely inviting someone to church, being kind, having spiritual conversations, or living as a good example. Those things matter, but evangelism happens when the Gospel is actually communicated. People need to hear who Jesus is, what He has done, and how they are called to respond.

Evangelism matters because people are eternal. The Bible is clear that all people have sinned, stand under judgment, and need salvation. This is not comfortable to say, but love requires honesty. If Jesus is the only Savior, then silence is not compassion. Christians do not share the Gospel because we are better than others. We share because we have received mercy and want others to receive mercy too.

The heart of evangelism is not winning arguments or proving that we are right. The heart of evangelism is bearing witness to Christ. Acts 1 says that Jesus sends His people as witnesses by the power of the Holy Spirit. A witness tells what is true. We testify that Jesus lived, died, rose again, and now offers forgiveness and life to all who repent and believe.

This means evangelism requires both courage and dependence. It takes courage to speak when the conversation might become awkward, when someone may disagree, or when we do not know exactly how they will respond. But evangelism also requires dependence because only God can save. We cannot argue someone into the kingdom. We cannot manipulate someone into faith. We speak the truth, pray for the Spirit’s work, and trust God with the results.

Faithful evangelism is both clear and personal. We are not giving a sales pitch. We are talking to real people with real stories, questions, wounds, assumptions, and beliefs. Good evangelism listens well. It asks honest questions. It seeks to understand. It does not change the Gospel, but it does consider the person in front of us.

This is why every Christian can grow in evangelism. Some people are naturally bold. Others are more relational, thoughtful, or quiet. Faithfulness may look different depending on personality, context, and opportunity, but every disciple is called to bear witness to Jesus. A student can share the Gospel with a roommate, coworker, classmate, teammate, international student, family member, or friend.

Evangelism also connects closely to the other focuses. We need to articulate the Gospel clearly. We need prayerful dependence on God. We need community that welcomes people in. We need love that makes the Gospel visible. But eventually, loving someone means speaking the good news clearly. Romans 10 asks, “How are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard?”

A healthy disciple looks for opportunities to move toward others with the Gospel. Not with pressure, arrogance, or fear, but with love, clarity, and courage. Jesus is worthy to be proclaimed, and people are worth pursuing. The good news is too good to keep to ourselves.

### Disciple

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: DISCIPLE]*

#### Definition

To disciple means to intentionally help others follow Jesus by pointing them to Scripture, modeling faithfulness, encouraging obedience, and walking with them over time.

#### Quotes

> “It is necessary, that we should know every person that belongeth to our charge.”
>
> — Richard Baxter, *The Reformed Pastor* (1656)

> “The Christian needs another Christian who speaks God’s Word to him.”
>
> — Dietrich Bonhoeffer, *Life Together*, 23 (1939)

> “Only as disciples were made could the other activities of the commission fulfill their purpose.”
>
> — Robert E. Coleman, *The Master Plan of Evangelism* (1963)

#### Article

Discipleship is helping others follow Jesus. Every Christian is first a disciple of Christ. A disciple is someone who trusts the Gospel, is being transformed by Jesus, walks with His people, and joins His mission. But disciples are also called to make disciples. The Christian life is not meant to end with us. God intends to use ordinary believers to help others know, trust, obey, and become more like Jesus.

Discipleship belongs under Mission because it is part of how the Gospel multiplies. Jesus did not simply command His followers to make converts. He commanded them to make disciples. This means evangelism and discipleship belong together. We want people to come to faith in Jesus, and we want them to grow into maturity in Jesus.

To disciple someone is not to make them dependent on us. It is not about building our own following, proving our spiritual maturity, or becoming someone’s savior. Discipleship is intentionally helping another person follow Christ. We point people to Scripture, pray with them, model repentance, speak truth, ask questions, encourage obedience, and walk with them over time.

This often happens through ordinary relationships. Discipleship can happen over coffee, in a Huddle, after church, in a dorm room, during a car ride, or through consistent conversations over months and years. It does not require someone to have all the answers. It requires faithfulness, humility, availability, and a desire to help another person take their next step toward Jesus.

Healthy discipleship is both relational and directional. It is relational because people are not projects. We genuinely love the people we disciple. We listen to them, know their lives, carry burdens with them, and care about them beyond a weekly meeting. But discipleship is also directional because we are helping one another move toward Christ. Christian friendship should be warm, but it should not be aimless.

Discipleship also includes modeling. Much of following Jesus is caught, not just taught. People learn by watching how we pray, confess sin, read Scripture, treat others, respond to suffering, share the Gospel, and make decisions. This does not mean we need to model perfection. In fact, one of the most important things we can model is repentance. A faithful discipler can say, “Follow me as I follow Christ,” while also honestly saying, “I still need grace too.”

The Gospel keeps discipleship humble. We do not disciple others because we are impressive. We disciple because Jesus has been patient with us, and because someone else has likely helped us grow. Any fruit that comes through discipleship belongs to God. We plant, water, teach, encourage, and pray, but God gives the growth.

Do not wait until you feel like an expert. Ask God to place one person on your heart: a younger believer, a new student, or a friend who needs help taking a step toward Christ. God delights to use ordinary, faithful people to help others know and follow Jesus. Discipleship does not have to be incredibly creative or done at the expert level to be faithful. Over time, God uses simple, intentional investment to form people who will turn around and help others follow Jesus too.

### Serve

*[FOCUS ARTWORK INSERT: SERVE]*

#### Definition

To serve means to use our lives, gifts, time, and energy for the good of others and the glory of God because Jesus has served us first.

#### Quotes

> “Let the strong take care of the weak; let the weak respect the strong. Let the rich man minister to the poor man; let the poor man give thanks to God that he gave him one through whom his need might be satisfied.”
>
> — Clement of Rome, *1 Clement*, c. 96 AD

> “Behold, from faith thus flow forth love and joy in the Lord, and from love a joyful, willing, and free mind that serves one’s neighbor willingly.”
>
> — Martin Luther, *The Freedom of a Christian*, 1520

> “The church is the body of people called by God’s grace through faith in Christ to glorify him together by serving him in his world.”
>
> — Mark Dever, *The Church*, 2012

#### Article

Serving is the faithful use of our lives, gifts, time, and energy for the good of others and the glory of God. Christians do not serve in order to prove their worth, earn God’s approval, or build a reputation. We serve because Jesus has served us first. The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.

Serving belongs under Mission because God uses His people to build up the church and bless the world. Mission is not only preaching, traveling, or leading. It also includes ordinary acts of faithfulness that help others encounter the love and truth of Christ. When believers serve on a Sunday, welcome new people, care for children, set up chairs, lead worship, make coffee, greet guests, or help meet practical needs, they participate in the mission of God.

The church is a body, and every member matters. 1 Corinthians 12 teaches that the body has many parts, and each part has a role to play. No Christian has every gift, but every Christian has some gift. God gives His people different abilities, experiences, resources, and passions so that the whole body would be built up. Serving is one way we refuse to live as spectators and instead take responsibility for the health of the church.

Serving also displays the Gospel. In a culture that often values status, comfort, and personal platform, Christian service points to a different kingdom. We follow a Savior who stooped low, washed feet, carried burdens, and gave Himself away. When we serve humbly and joyfully, we reflect something of His heart.

This does not mean serving is always easy. Serving can be inconvenient, unseen, repetitive, and tiring. Sometimes we will not be thanked. Sometimes we will have to do things that do not feel important. Sometimes we will need to serve when we would rather be served. But hidden faithfulness matters to God. He sees what others do not.

Serving also requires wisdom. We are not called to say yes to everything or burn ourselves out trying to meet every need. Faithful service is not the same as frantic service. Faithful service is generous and honest about our limits, seasons, and responsibilities. The goal is not exhaustion. The goal is faithfulness.

When we see how freely Christ has served us, serving others becomes more than a duty. It becomes a joyful response to grace. We begin looking for needs, taking the lower place, and giving our lives for the good of others. This may happen in the gathered church, in a Huddle, on a mission trip, in a home, on a campus, or in a workplace. The question is not simply, “What do I want to do?” but “How has God gifted me, and where can I build up others?”

Finally, the Gospel frees us to serve without needing to be noticed. In Christ, we are already loved, already accepted, and already secure. That means we can take the lower place, meet practical needs, and give ourselves for others with joy. We serve because Jesus served us.
